\title{An AI-Aware Security Gateway for Retrieval-Augmented Chatbots:\\
Model-Independent Controls and a Cross-Model Evaluation}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Daniel Martins Ferreira, Rui Machado, Henrique Teixeira,
Sebasti\~ao Teixeira, and Osvaldo Pacheco}
\IEEEauthorblockA{IEETA, LASI, University of Aveiro, Aveiro, Portugal\\
\{danielmartinsferreira, rmachado, henriqueft04, sebastiao.teixeira, orp\}@ua.pt}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets chatbots answer from an organisation's
own documents, but untrusted retrieved content and user input then reach the model
and its tools directly, opening the door to prompt injection, retrieval-time data
leakage, and tool abuse. The usual defence trusts the model's safety alignment, a
property of one component that an operator can neither inspect nor guarantee. We
instead treat injected content as a fault to be contained rather than detected, and
place the security-relevant decisions in a gateway around the model: a Policy
Enforcement Point on the RAG pipeline with five controls, two structural (retrieval
authorisation, scoped tools) that hold by construction for every model, a
partially-structural context quarantine, and two heuristic (input filtering, output
redaction) for defence in depth. We map the controls onto the OWASP Top 10 for LLM
Applications, integrate the gateway with the document store and identity provider of
ROOTS, an operational tourism-sustainability platform, as a feasibility exercise, and evaluate it on a controlled corpus
across a deterministic worst case and six open-source models (7B--70B). With the
gateway on, no attack in six scenarios produces its scored effect on any model, while
the guardrails-off baseline varies from model to model; a factorial sweep attributes
each closure to a specific control rather than to the hardened prompt, with the two
structural controls closing the authorisation and agency attacks on their own, and the
gateway covers classes that content-safety classifiers are not built to see. On 200
benign queries across five models the gateway rejects four near-miss inputs and
alters no answer; the gateway never withholds a restricted document from an entitled user (60 of 60
retrievals), the model states the figure in 40 of those, and redaction masks literal
credential strings in five. The
structural guarantees hold by construction; the empirical claims cover open-source
models with lexical retrieval and comment-embedded indirect payloads.
\end{abstract}

RAG security, prompt injection, LLM guardrails, OWASP LLM Top-10, AI gateway,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become the default way to put a large
language model (LLM) to work over an organisation's own documents
\cite{lewis2020rag}: a user question is answered not from the model's weights
alone but from passages retrieved at query time from a private corpus. That design
is also where the security model breaks down, because two untrusted inputs now
reach the model and any tools it can call, namely the user's message and the
content of the documents the retriever returns. An attacker who controls either
one can try to override the system prompt, read documents they are not entitled
to, or drive a connected tool to act on their behalf. Direct and indirect prompt
injection, sensitive-information disclosure, and excessive agency sit among the
most prominent entries in the Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) Top 10 for LLM Applications
\cite{owasp2025llmtop10,greshake2023indirect,perez2022ignore}.

The common response is to rely on the model's own safety alignment to refuse
malicious instructions, but that is the wrong layer to depend on. Alignment is a
property of a specific model, it varies between models and versions, and an
operator can neither inspect nor guarantee it; a control that holds for one
open-source model may fail on the next, with nothing in the deployment making the
difference visible. From a dependability standpoint the model is a component whose
failure modes under adversarial input are unknown and unverifiable, and a system that
relies on such a component to enforce its own security policy has no containment
boundary. We take the opposite position and place the security-relevant decisions in
a gateway around the model rather than in the model itself. The gateway sits on the
RAG pipeline as a Policy Enforcement Point and applies five controls regardless of
which model produces the answer. Two are least-privilege guarantees that hold by
their position in the pipeline, a third is partially structural, and the remaining
two are best-effort heuristic checks for defence in depth. Retrieval authorisation
and scoped tools are standard access-control practices, least privilege applied to
retrieval and tool use \cite{saltzer1975protection}; rather than inventing controls,
we compose them into a model-independent gateway and measure, across models, how
much safety comes from the gateway versus from a given model's alignment.

We evaluate six attack scenarios over five OWASP classes: S1 direct prompt injection, S2 indirect injection through a retrieved document, S3 RAG data leakage,
S4 tool abuse, S5 system-prompt leakage, and S6 improper output handling. The paper
makes three contributions: a model-independent gateway of five controls with a threat model and security properties that hold by construction (Sections~\ref{sec:threat}--\ref{sec:owasp}); a
feasibility integration of the gateway with the help assistant of ROOTS, the tourism-sustainability observatory of the ATT agenda at the University of Aveiro, wired to its document store and identity provider
(Section~\ref{sec:deployment}); and a cross-model evaluation attributing the closure of each attack to a specific
control, with the structural pair closing data leakage and tool abuse regardless of
prompt (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). That evaluation pairs the model-dependent
guardrails-off baseline against the uniform gateway-on behaviour and adds a full
$2^5$ factorial sweep, external guardrail baselines, two scorer-validity checks,
a negligible-overhead analysis, and a utility study: over-blocking on \fpN{} benign
queries across five models and an entitled-user access check. The evaluation code,
rubric, corpus, and every stored transcript are in the public
artifact.\footnote{Code, rubric, corpus, transcripts, and data: \artifacturl.}

\section{Background and Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

A RAG chatbot answers a user message $m$ from a corpus $D$ of documents. A
retriever selects a small set $R \subseteq D$ relevant to $m$, the model generates
a response from the system prompt, $m$, and the text of $R$, and the response may
request calls to external tools. Both $m$ and the content of $R$ are
attacker-influenced, so every stage downstream of retrieval is an opportunity for
the attacker.

We model the deployment with users $U$ and documents $D$. Each user $u$ holds
clearances $\mathit{cl}(u)\subseteq C$ and each document $d$ a label
$\mathit{acl}(d)\in C$; $u$ may read $d$ only if $\mathit{acl}(d)\in\mathit{cl}(u)$,
and we write $A(u)=\{d\in D:\mathit{acl}(d)\in\mathit{cl}(u)\}$ for the documents $u$
is entitled to. The system prompt $\mathit{sys}$ and the gateway configuration are
trusted developer artefacts. The user message $m$ and the body of every retrieved
document are untrusted data: they may inform an answer but must never be promoted to
instructions.

The attacker aims to disclose a restricted document, execute an out-of-scope action,
exfiltrate the system prompt, or emit active content, and has two capabilities:
(C1) it submits arbitrary user messages, including ones that try to override the
system prompt or drive the tools (direct injection); and (C2) it plants arbitrary
content in documents the retriever may later surface, so that retrieved text carries
instructions (indirect injection and corpus
poisoning)~\cite{greshake2023indirect,zou2024poisonedrag}. It controls neither the
gateway configuration nor the access labels.

This is the confused-deputy problem~\cite{hardy1988confused}. Without the gateway
the model is a deputy that acts on whatever text reaches it, exercising the union of
the developer's authority (its tools) and any authority implied by untrusted content;
injected text borrows that authority. The gateway re-imposes the principal's
authority at the pipeline boundary, binding retrieval to $\mathit{cl}(u)$ before the
deputy acts and tool execution to a fixed scope after it, so that a misled deputy cannot exceed the developer's tool scope or the user's read entitlements; it can still misuse authority the user legitimately holds, a residual we make explicit in Section~\ref{ssec:nonguar}.

The guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:properties} rest on three assumptions, stated so
their scope is explicit. (A1) Access labels and the principal-to-clearance binding
$\mathit{cl}(u)$ are authentic and tamper-proof, enforced by the document store and the identity provider (in deployment, by verifying the caller's token against the realm's published signing keys, its JSON Web Key Set or JWKS); if either can be forged, retrieval authorisation no longer holds.
(A2) For the heuristic controls only, the attacker is non-adaptive: one who knows
the filters' patterns can craft obfuscated bypasses, and such evasion is out of
scope for P1 and P4, though not for the structural controls, which never inspect
content. (A3) For the quarantine guarantee, the model honours the untrusted-data
fence; we make this precise in Section~\ref{sec:properties} and show the reference
gateway satisfies it by construction.

In dependability terms~\cite{avizienis2004taxonomy}, injected content is a
malicious external fault; a model that acts on it is a component whose internal
failure modes are unknown, and the failure we want to prevent is an unauthorised
disclosure or action at the system boundary. The gateway is an error-containment
boundary around that component: it does not try to detect the fault inside the model
but confines what an erroneous model output can reach, in the tradition of the
reference monitor~\cite{anderson1972computer} and of execution monitors that enforce
a policy over observable events~\cite{schneider2000enforceable}. The evaluation of
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} is then a fault-injection campaign: one representative
fault per class, injected through both channels, with and without the containment
boundary.

The gateway enforces the five controls of Table~\ref{tab:props}: two structural (P2, P5), a partially-structural quarantine (P3), and two heuristic (P1, P4). The next
two sections present the design and then prove which guarantees hold for every model.

\section{The AI-Aware Security Gateway}
\label{sec:gateway}

The gateway is a single pipeline parameterised by a security policy
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gateway}). In the guardrails-off configuration every control is off
and a weak, secret-bearing system prompt is used, so the user message and
retrieved content flow straight to the model and its tools; in the gateway-on configuration all five controls are on and a hardened prompt is used. Expressing
the difference as one policy object rather than as branches in the code keeps the
before/after comparison exact and makes the gateway a drop-in component for any RAG
application. The controls act in pipeline order: the input filter rejects an
injecting message before retrieval; retrieval authorisation restricts the
candidate set to documents the user may read, so a restricted document is never
placed in the context; context quarantine fences what is retrieved; the model then
generates; output redaction strips secret and active content on the way out; and
scoped tools validate every requested call before executing it.

The design claim is that P2 and P5 do not depend on the model. Retrieval
authorisation filters candidates before the model runs, so even a model that fully
obeys an injected instruction cannot disclose a document it never received, and a
scoped tool refuses an out-of-policy call even if the model emits it verbatim.
Whether a model refuses a naive ``ignore your instructions'' prompt is, by
contrast, an alignment property that varies across models. The evaluation
quantifies both halves of this picture and, through the factorial sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, attributes the gateway-on result to the controls
rather than to the hardened prompt and isolates the marginal effect of each
individual control.

The gateway ships as a library configured by the policy object, with four pluggable
interfaces (a labelled corpus, a principals source, a model provider, an optional
judge); Section~\ref{sec:deployment} binds the first two to an operational platform
while the evaluation swaps only the provider across seven backends, with the control
implementations byte-for-byte identical throughout.

\section{Security Properties}
\label{sec:properties}

We give the gateway a semantics that makes precise which guarantees hold for every
model and which are conditional. A model is any function $M$ mapping an input triple
$(\mathit{sys},m,X)$ to a response $r$ and a set of tool calls $\mathcal{C}$. We
quantify over $M$, so a statement proved for all $M$ depends on no model's
alignment. On a request $(u,m)$ the gateway-on pipeline retrieves
$R=\mathit{topk}(m,A(u))\subseteq A(u)$, fences it as $X=\mathit{quarantine}(R)$,
computes $(r,\mathcal{C})=M(\mathit{sys},m,X)$, releases $r'=\mathit{redact}(r)$, and
executes $\mathit{Exec}=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:\mathit{name}(c)\in\mathit{allow}\wedge
\mathit{args}(c)\in\mathit{scope}\}$. The quarantine transform wraps each chunk in an
untrusted-data fence, strips HTML comments, and neutralises embedded control
markers.

\subsection{Model-independent guarantees}

\begin{proposition}[P2, retrieval-authorisation soundness]
\label{prop:p2}
For every model $M$, user $u$, and message $m$, the model input contains content
only from documents in $A(u)$; consequently no document $d$ with
$\mathit{acl}(d)\notin\mathit{cl}(u)$ influences the response.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By A1 the access labels and the user's clearances $\mathit{cl}(u)$ are authentic, so
$A(u)$ is the true authorised set.
Retrieval restricts candidates to $A(u)$ before $M$ is invoked, so $R\subseteq A(u)$
and $M$'s input is a function only of $(\mathit{sys},m,\mathit{quarantine}(R))$. No
$d\notin A(u)$ appears in that input, for any $M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[P5, tool-scope soundness]
\label{prop:p5}
For every model $M$ and output $(r,\mathcal{C})$, a call $c$ is executed only if
$\mathit{name}(c)\in\mathit{allow}$ and $\mathit{args}(c)\in\mathit{scope}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\mathit{Exec}$ filters $\mathcal{C}$ by the scope predicate before execution,
independent of how $\mathcal{C}$ was produced.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[P3, quarantine completeness]
\label{prop:p3c}
For every model $M$, user $u$, and message $m$, every retrieved chunk reaching the
model is enclosed in the untrusted-data fence and carries no marker from the set
that $\mathit{quarantine}$ neutralises.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The pipeline applies $\mathit{quarantine}$ to every element of $R$ before forming
$X$; by construction $\mathit{quarantine}$ wraps its argument in the fence and
removes the neutralised markers, independent of $M$.
\end{proof}

Propositions~\ref{prop:p2}--\ref{prop:p3c} hold by the position of the control in
the pipeline, before $M$ (P2, P3) or after it (P5), which is the precise sense in
which they are model-independent; their proofs restate the pipeline, and that is the
point: nothing about $M$ enters them. Their value for an implementer is the list of
conditions under which the restatement is faithful: the candidate set must be
computed under the requesting user's clearances rather than a service account's;
every input the model sees, including conversation history and cached retrievals,
must pass through the same filter and fence; and the audit trail that records a
filtered document must go to the operator's log, never back to the caller, or P2
would itself disclose that a matching restricted record exists. P5 bounds the
\emph{shape} of an executed call, its name and arguments lie in scope; it does not
bound the information a within-scope call may carry (Section~\ref{ssec:nonguar}).

\subsection{A conditional guarantee for quarantine}

Quarantine completeness is structural, but whether fencing actually stops an
embedded instruction depends on the model honouring the fence. We isolate that
dependence in a single assumption. The fenced region may legitimately inform the
answer; what must not happen is for it to change the tool calls the model makes.

\begin{definition}[Fence-respecting model]
\label{def:fence}
$M$ is \emph{fence-respecting} if the tool calls it emits are invariant under changes
confined to the fenced region of its context: for any $X,X'$ that agree outside the
fence, $M(\mathit{sys},m,X)$ and $M(\mathit{sys},m,X')$ emit the same tool calls
$\mathcal{C}$. The free-text answer $r$ may differ, as it may legitimately use the
fenced content.
\end{definition}

Since every retrieved chunk lies inside the fence, the definition says that the
model's tool calls do not depend on retrieved content at all. That is a strong
assumption: it excludes legitimate retrieval-dependent tool use (e-mailing the
customer named in a retrieved ticket) and characterises the action-selector regime
of the design-pattern catalogue~\cite{beurerkellner2025designpatterns}, in which tool
arguments never come from retrieved data. The proposition that follows is then
immediate; we state it because it names exactly what A3 buys.

\begin{proposition}[P3, quarantine soundness under A3]
\label{prop:p3s}
If $M$ is fence-respecting, then no content in a retrieved document changes $M$'s
tool calls: a tool call the request would not otherwise emit cannot be induced
through retrieved content.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:p3c} every retrieved chunk reaching $M$ lies inside the
fence. Replacing the embedded content with inert text is a change confined to the
fenced region, so by Definition~\ref{def:fence} the tool calls are unchanged.
\end{proof}

The reference gateway realises a fence-respecting model by construction: its
deterministic backend applies a fence-stripping step that removes the fenced region
before scanning for commands, so its tool calls cannot depend on fenced content and
Assumption A3 holds exactly. The stub is therefore the alignment worst case (it never
refuses) but the \emph{best} case for quarantine: it never reads fenced text for
instructions, so its gateway-on result on the indirect scenarios follows from its own
construction, and the corresponding result for real models rests on the marker
stripping of Proposition~\ref{prop:p3c} and on where the payload sits. Its results
are not an upper bound on compromise for a model that violates A3, which is why P5
caps tool execution and P3 is only partially structural. Whether a production model satisfies
A3 is an empirical question; we pair P3 with output redaction (P4), which is what
closes S5-style disclosure rather than this conditional guarantee.

\subsection{Non-guarantees: the heuristic controls}
\label{ssec:nonguar}

The input filter P1 rejects a message matching a finite injection-pattern set $\Pi$,
and output redaction P4 substitutes a finite set $\Sigma$ of secret and
active-content shapes in the response. Neither is sound. For any finite $\Pi$ there
are semantically equivalent messages outside it (paraphrase, homoglyph substitution,
instruction splitting), and for any finite $\Sigma$ there are secret or
active-content encodings it does not match; an attacker who knows the patterns can
evade them, which is Assumption A2 made explicit. P1 and P4 are therefore defence in
depth, not guarantees. The structural controls bound the damage regardless: by
Propositions~\ref{prop:p2} and~\ref{prop:p5} an attacker who evades P1 and P4 still
cannot cause the model to receive an unauthorised document or to execute an
out-of-scope tool call. Scoped tools share this character: P5 refuses any
out-of-scope call, but a within-scope call (internal recipient, body within the cap)
can still carry bounded data, so P5 limits rather than eliminates tool-mediated
disclosure; the S4 result measures the external-recipient attack P5 provably
refuses. The residual is a cross-principal confused deputy: an entitled HR user who
reads a public document carrying ``e-mail the compensation bands to the support
desk'' satisfies P2, an
internal recipient on the allow-listed domain satisfies P5, and if the model violates
A3 the restricted content reaches a mailbox without clearance. Binding the recipient
set to the clearances of the documents in context would close it and is on our
agenda. This division of labour is the design principle of the gateway: the
heuristics reduce incidence, the structural controls cap impact.

\section{Integration with an Operational Platform}
\label{sec:deployment}

The target application is the help assistant of ROOTS, a microservice system whose knowledge-base service exposes documentation
articles over a REST API and whose identity is managed by a Keycloak realm
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Authenticated platform users (visitors, analysts, and
administrators, expressed as realm roles) ask natural-language questions about the
platform's indicators, data sources, and documentation; the assistant answers from
the knowledge-base articles and may call one action tool, sending a message on the
user's behalf, whose recipient set and body size the scoped-tool control bounds. A
new chatbot microservice imports the gateway as a library and sits behind the
platform's nginx reverse proxy on a dedicated route; each request carries the
user's bearer token and returns the answer; the gateway's audit trail (retrieved
document identifiers, control decisions, tool results) belongs in the operator's log,
since returning it to the caller would reveal which restricted records matched, and
an operator switch selects the gateway-on or guardrails-off policy for
demonstrations. The service binds
the gateway's two deployment interfaces to the platform: the corpus interface loads
documents from the knowledge-base REST API, and the principals interface validates
the caller's Keycloak bearer token against the realm's JWKS and maps the realm roles
it carries to gateway clearances (every caller implicitly holds \texttt{public}), so
retrieval authorisation (P2) runs on real identities. A document's access label is
carried by an \texttt{acl} field added to the knowledge-base record schema. Only
these two adapters are platform-specific; the five control implementations are the
same code exercised in the evaluation. This demonstrates that the gateway slots into
a real document store and identity provider without changes to the control logic.
It is an integration and feasibility result: the adapters are exercised by the
service's test suite against the stub model, no end-to-end traffic was sent through
the deployed stack, and we report no operational metrics, latency, or real-user
study from it.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}
The question is whether composing the controls yields model-independent safety,
and how the guardrails-off baseline varies across models. We run the six scenarios
in two modes, guardrails-off (every control off, the baseline) and gateway-on (all
five controls and the hardened prompt), over a deterministic stub model and six open-source instruct models:
Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen2.5~7B, Mistral~7B, Gemma~3~12B, and Phi-4~14B served locally
through Ollama, and Llama~3.1~70B on a rented A40 GPU. The main grid covers all six
models in both modes. The factorial sweep, dose-response, temperature-0.7,
scoring-validity, and utility analyses that follow cover the five locally-served
models (7B--14B) and the stub; the remotely-served 70B enters only the main
single-run grid as a scale spot-check, without the per-cell transcripts, judge
re-scoring, or annotation the other models carry, so its rows are indicative rather
than audited. Decoding is greedy (temperature~0, fixed seed) so each cell is
deterministic and reported directly; a temperature-0.7 run later checks that the
findings survive sampling. The stub is a naive instruction-follower with no refusal
mechanism that echoes whatever an injection asks for, fixing the worst case and
making the controls' effect reproducible. We report, per (model, scenario, mode),
the attack success rate (ASR), the share of cells in which the scenario's scored
effect occurred (a secret string or scam text released, a restricted document
disclosed, an e-mail sent off the internal domain, active content emitted), and note
refusals (the model declined, an alignment signal). The controls do not all register as an explicit block: P1 and P5 hard-reject observably, whereas P2 and P3 prevent the outcome by suppression (the
unauthorised document or instruction never reaches the model), which shows up as
reduced ASR rather than a separate signal. Outcomes are scored by a transparent
deterministic rubric keyed to each scenario's markers and specified in the released
code. The scoped tool's allow-list is the single configured internal domain and a
512-byte body cap; every recipient the models invented (finance, HR, help desk on a
guessed domain) lies outside it, so P5 refused every gateway-on call in this grid and
its accept path is exercised only by the unit tests.

\subsection{Model-independent safety and the alignment baseline}
With the gateway on, ASR is zero by the rubric for every model on every scenario, meaning that no scenario's scored effect occurred; the scoring-validity check below shows where human readers scored model compliance more strictly than executed effect.
This uniform closure is an empirical observation about the full gateway, not a single
guarantee: only data leakage (S3) and tool abuse (S4) follow from the
model-independent structural controls (P2, P5); direct injection (S1) is caught by the
input filter or redaction, system-prompt leakage (S5) by the hardened prompt and
redaction, and the indirect attacks (S2, S6) by quarantine, which on the evaluated
payloads strips the embedded markers structurally
(Proposition~\ref{prop:p3c}); the conditional fence-respecting guarantee
(Proposition~\ref{prop:p3s}) governs only a residual plain-text directive that
survives stripping. The structural part holds for every model by construction; the
cross-model data is what makes the rest credible. With guardrails off the picture
inverts (Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap}): the unaligned stub is compromised on all six
scenarios, while the six aligned models are compromised inconsistently, with mean
guardrails-off ASR ranging from $0.17$ (Llama~3.1~8B, Phi-4~14B) to $0.50$
(Mistral~7B). The variation is attack-specific rather than a clean model ranking,
since only data leakage (S3) and tool abuse (S4) differ across models while S1, S2,
and S6 produce their scored effect on none of them. That is not the same as
refusal: five models refuse S1 but Mistral~7B complies without echoing the secret,
and on S2 and S6 most models answer or draft e-mails rather than refuse
(Table~\ref{tab:sessions}). Refusals occur in 11 of the 36 instrumented guardrails-off cells (stub and five local
models) and 8 of the 36 gateway-on cells, the 70B refused four of its six
guardrails-off cells, and refusal and leak co-occur: Phi-4~14B states the override
code inside its S5 refusal, and Gemma~3~12B states it inside its S4 refusal. Scale did not buy safety on this payload:
Llama~3.1~70B (mean $0.33$) disclosed the restricted document on S3 in its single run
where its 8B sibling refused. System-prompt leakage (S5) succeeds in guardrails-off
mode on every instrumented model and on the 70B spot-check, because the oblique
request slips past the alignment that refuses the blatant variant (S1). The gap
between the two modes is the safety the gateway adds on top of whatever alignment a
model happens to provide, and it is the same gap for every model. The greedy zeros on S1, S2, and S6 are decoding artefacts: re-running the
guardrails-off baseline at temperature 0.7 with ten seeded samples per cell preserves
the cross-model variation (mean per-model ASR $0.07$--$0.60$) and surfaces direct
injection in 1--3 of 10 samples on Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen2.5~7B, and Mistral~7B, the S2
payload in 1 of 10 on Qwen2.5~7B and Gemma~3~12B, and the S6 script in 3 of 10 on
Mistral~7B; P1 and P3 close all of these regardless of decoding, and the sampled sweep
covers the baseline only. One scope note applies to the indirect scenarios throughout:
all three planted payloads sit in HTML comments, which quarantine strips; a payload in
a document's visible body would reach the model fenced but intact, and its closure
would then rest on A3 and on P4.

\subsection{Scoring validity}
Two scorer checks test whether the rubric's zero is an artefact of its markers (the
prompt and the decoder are checked by the factorial and temperature sweeps below).
Two annotators from the author team independently labelled the 72 instrumented cells
(stub and the five local models, six scenarios, two modes) from the stored outputs
with a binary attack/no-attack label, blind to the rubric's label. They agree with
each other on 67 (93\%; Cohen's $\kappa$ = \humankappa), but with the rubric on only
60 and 55 of 72 respectively, and the disagreements are informative. Both annotators
scored five gateway-on cells as successful attacks that the rubric scores as none:
three Mistral~7B cells in which the model emitted an e-mail request that P5 refused,
and two cells (stub and Phi-4 on S4) in which the model narrated processing the
invoice without calling any tool. The annotators scored the model's \emph{compliance}
with the injected intent; the rubric scores the \emph{executed effect}. The same reading raises the baseline: both annotators also scored five
guardrails-off cells the rubric passes (Qwen2.5 and Phi-4 narrating the S4 payment,
Mistral's S2 and S6 e-mails, and Gemma~3 stating the override code inside its S4
refusal, a disclosure the S4-keyed rubric does not look for) and Mistral's S1
compliance without the secret, and scored Mistral's guardrails-off S4 e-mail to the
vendor as none where the rubric counts any off-domain send. By the compliance reading
the gap is 20 of 36 guardrails-off cells against 5 of 36 gateway-on for one annotator
and 10 of 36 for the other, who also counted four secret-free prompt echoes on S5 and
one hedged S3 non-answer as partial disclosure; those five cells are the whole of the
inter-annotator disagreement. Every gateway-on success under either reading is a call
P5 refused, a narration without a tool call, or an echo of a secret-free prompt: the
model was misled, the boundary held. As the second check we
re-scored every stored output with an LLM judge (Llama~3.1~8B reading each transcript
and the scenario goal); judge and rubric agree on 63 of the 72 cells (88\%,
Table~\ref{tab:scorer}), the judge assigns no gateway-on success, and the nine
off-diagonal cells split into four disclosure calls (in three of which the transcript
carries the restricted figures or the override code verbatim, so the judge and not the
rubric erred), four judgements on emitted e-mails, and one ``misleading response''. The judge is a same-family cross-check
rather than an independent one, as Llama~3.1~8B is itself an evaluated model and
backs the NeMo baseline. The human labels are released with the artifact.

\subsection{Attribution: which lever closes which attack}
The factorial sweep separates the prompt from the controls
(Table~\ref{tab:factorial}). Hardening the prompt alone leaves the structural
attacks open: data leakage and tool abuse stay open until retrieval
authorisation and scoped tools are added, which close them regardless of the
prompt, whereas system-prompt leakage is closed by the hardened prompt (which simply
carries no secret) and not by P2 or P5 (the one-sixth dip under the structural pair
alone is a single backend that happened not to echo the prompt in that greedy run,
not a closure); the residual $0.17$ in the hardened-prompt column is the
worst-case stub, which the prompt cannot help and which only output redaction closes.
The hardened prompt does not help data leakage (S3 moves from $0.67$ to $0.83$, one
model flipping in a single greedy run), which is the case for a structural control
rather than prompt engineering, and it has a side effect of its own: on S6 it raises
ASR from $0.17$ to $0.33$ because Mistral~7B, which describes the promotion under the
weak prompt, emits the planted script under the hardened one and keeps doing so with
P1 or P2 added; only quarantine or redaction removes it. The two levers act on different scenarios, and the
structural controls are the ones that act where alignment fails. A full $2^5$
sweep over every control combination isolates each control (single-control rows are
in the artifact): on the worst-case stub, enabling one control at a time closes each
attack with the control mapped to it, and system-prompt leakage is closed only by
output redaction, since the oblique request bypasses the input filter. A pre-redaction re-scoring supports the same reading: with the
gateway on, the model's output before the filter is already clean for every real
model on S5 and S6, so quarantine and the hardened prompt prevent the leak upstream
and the output filter is only a backstop, relied on solely by the naive stub (with
the weak prompt and all five controls, four real models leak S5 before redaction and
P4 alone saves them).

\subsection{Comparison with off-the-shelf guardrails}
Two off-the-shelf guardrail systems cover far less of this surface
(Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline}), and the comparison is about coverage, not accuracy.
Llama Guard~3~\cite{inan2023llamaguard} is a content-safety classifier over a
hazard taxonomy, not an injection detector; it flags none of the six attack inputs,
which read as benign, and three of the six worst-case outputs, missing data leakage,
tool abuse, and prompt leakage. NeMo Guardrails'~\cite{rebedea2023nemo} self-check
input rail, backed by Llama~3.1~8B, blocks only the blatant direct injection. Neither
is designed for S3, S4, or S5, whose inputs are benign by construction, and that is
the point: an input or output classifier has nothing to classify where the attack is
an authorisation or agency failure, so such attacks need a control that acts on the
candidate set and on the call, not on the text. A like-for-like injection detector
(Meta's Prompt Guard) would be the fair accuracy comparison on S1, S2, and S6 and is
future work.

\subsection{Operational cost}
The controls are pure functions of the request, so the gateway adds negligible latency.
Timing each control in isolation over repeated calls on the deterministic backend gives medians of
$0.3$--$0.9\,\mu$s for the input filter, retrieval authorisation, quarantine, and
tool validation, and $5.3\,\mu$s for output redaction (the most regex-heavy); the five together cost about $7.5\,\mu$s per query, over five
orders of magnitude below the per-model median inference time of
$1.5$--$7.6$\,s we measured (Llama~3.1~8B to Phi-4~14B). The timing covers the
controls' own logic; in the integration, token verification against the identity
provider and label lookups against the document store add network round trips we
did not measure.



\subsection{Utility: over-blocking and entitled access}
\label{ssec:utility}
A security layer that blocks legitimate use is not deployable, so we measure the
utility cost from two sides. The first is over-blocking by the two
content-inspection controls, which can in principle fire on benign traffic. We built
a corpus of \fpN{} benign questions a user of such a chatbot might ask, spanning local-services queries, IT help-desk questions answerable from the public articles, routine HR and account questions that request no restricted data, and
general customer service; \fpNearMiss{} of them are deliberate near-misses that use, in innocent senses,
vocabulary the injection or secret patterns key on (``booking system'', ``follow the
instructions on the sign'', ``ignore the rain forecast'', ``the system prompts me'',
``the secret to a good espresso''). Every question was run
through the gateway-on pipeline on each of the five locally-served models. The input filter is deterministic and model-independent: it rejected \fpPoneCount{}
of \fpN{} questions, all of them near-misses that reproduce one of its phrases
verbatim (``maintenance mode'' twice, ``the system prompts me'', ``you are now
offering''), while the other 65 near-misses passed, so its cost is a 2\% rejection
rate concentrated on messages that echo the filter's own vocabulary, each answered
with a rephrase request rather than silently dropped. Output redaction altered
\fpPfourTotal{} of the \fpPfourRuns{} answers the models produced for the remaining
196 questions (196 answers per model, no model call failed), so the table this
result would fill is all zeros and we report it as a sentence. The corpus is single-site and author-written, so we read this as
evidence that the patterns are not trigger-happy on realistic traffic rather than as a
precision guarantee; a deployment would measure the same two rates on its own logs,
and because P1 is heuristic defence in depth (Section~\ref{ssec:nonguar}) an operator
can narrow or drop a pattern without touching the structural guarantees.



The second side is whether retrieval authorisation over-blocks users who are
entitled to a restricted document, since P2 filters the candidate set for everyone.
We posed \fpUtilN{} legitimate questions whose correct answer requires a restricted
record (the executive-compensation bands and the production-credentials note) as an
entitled principal (the HR user for the compensation record, the security operator
for the credentials record) and as the \texttt{public}-only support agent, on the
stub and the five models. For the entitled principal the restricted document was
retrieved and placed in the context in \fpUtilRetrieved{} of \fpUtilRuns{} runs; for
the support agent it was retrieved in none, as Proposition~\ref{prop:p2} requires
(one generic question about the review cadence drew a plausible answer from general
knowledge on four models, with the record absent from the context). The gateway
therefore never withheld an entitled document. What the entitled user then received
depended on the layers behind it. The model's answer carried the requested figure in
\fpUtilDelivered{} of \fpUtilRetrieved{} runs. Of the rest, \fpUtilRefused{} are the
model itself declining, on confidentiality grounds, to state figures to a user
entitled to them (\fpUtilRefusedByModel{}), an over-refusal by the model's alignment
that the gateway neither causes nor can remove; \fpUtilTool{} are Mistral~7B
deflecting into an e-mail to the HR or security team instead of answering (refused by
P5, since it addressed a domain outside the allow-list); and \fpUtilYear{} are
comprehension failures on the two questions that name the year 2025, where three
models declared the year unavailable although the record's title carries it. Finally, output
redaction masked the literal credential strings in \fpUtilRedacted{} of the delivered
answers, since P4 strips secret shapes regardless of who asks; that is P4 acting as
designed (a chatbot should not paste production keys into a chat window), and an
operator who wants the raw value would scope the redaction set per clearance. The two
utility costs thus separate cleanly: containment by the gateway costs the entitled
user nothing on retrieval and only the literal secret string on output, while the
model-dependent cost, over-refusal, is one more reason not to make alignment the
enforcement layer.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Prompt injection was characterised by Perez and Ribeiro \cite{perez2022ignore} for
the direct case and by Greshake et al.\ \cite{greshake2023indirect} for the indirect
case, where instructions hidden in retrieved content hijack an LLM-integrated
application; HouYi \cite{liu2024houyi} turned this into a black-box attack framework
against deployed applications, and Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2024promptinjection} give the
first systematic cross-model benchmark. The surface has since been charted by
crowd-sourced attack corpora \cite{toyer2024tensortrust}, indirect-injection
benchmarks \cite{yi2025bipia}, and automatic, transferable payloads produced by
optimisation \cite{liu2024universalinjection}. These works define the classes the gateway must intercept.

One line of defence hardens the model itself. StruQ \cite{chen2024struq} and SecAlign
\cite{chen2024secalign} separate instructions from data through structured queries
and preference optimisation, spotlighting marks provenance \cite{hines2024spotlighting},
Jatmo \cite{piet2024jatmo} fine-tunes a model to its task so injected instructions are
not followed, and the instruction hierarchy \cite{wallace2024instructionhierarchy}
trains a model to prioritise privileged messages. These defences are per-model and re-trainable, and alignment can be shallow~\cite{qi2024shallowsafety} or countered only at the representation level~\cite{zou2024circuitbreakers}; the gateway's controls sit outside the model, so the same code applies unchanged across backends.

A second line constrains the system around the model. Design-pattern catalogues
\cite{beurerkellner2025designpatterns} and the dual-LLM pattern
\cite{willison2023dualllm} isolate untrusted content from privileged action, while
CaMeL \cite{debenedetti2025camel}, information-flow control \cite{wu2024fsecure},
type-directed privilege separation \cite{jacob2025typedirected}, and execution
isolation \cite{wu2025isolategpt} enforce least privilege by construction. The
gateway shares this secure-by-design stance but targets the RAG
retrieval-and-generation path with lightweight controls and reports a guardrails-off
baseline that quantifies what the structural layer adds.

RAG makes the corpus a first-class attack surface: the datastore can be extracted
through injected instructions~\cite{qi2025spillbeans}, probed for
membership~\cite{anderson2025ismydata,zeng2024goodbad}, poisoned~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag},
or backdoored~\cite{chaudhari2024phantom}; SafeRAG~\cite{liang2025saferag} benchmarks
pipelines against such attacks, and model self-critique~\cite{asai2024selfrag}
complements rather than replaces mediation at the retrieval boundary.

Tool-using agents widen the surface again: InjecAgent~\cite{zhan2024injecagent},
AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, and AgentHarm~\cite{andriushchenko2025agentharm}
benchmark injection and harmful tool use, and adaptive attacks break single-layer
agent defences~\cite{zhan2025adaptiveipi}, the empirical case for structural controls
that do not inspect content. Guardrail classifiers such as NeMo Guardrails
\cite{rebedea2023nemo}, Llama Guard \cite{inan2023llamaguard}, and Granite Guardian
\cite{padhi2024graniteguardian}, and adaptive security services \cite{pfister2025gandalf},
operate alongside such a layer rather than replacing it, as our baseline comparison
shows. We align the threat model with the OWASP LLM Top-10 \cite{owasp2025llmtop10},
the NIST AI Risk Management Framework~\cite{nist2023airmf}, and MITRE ATLAS \cite{mitre2024atlas}.
In the vocabulary of the pattern catalogue~\cite{beurerkellner2025designpatterns},
P2 is context minimisation at the retrieval boundary, P3 marks provenance, P5 is an
action-selector guard, and P1 and P4 are input and output filters; the contribution
is not a new pattern but a measured composition of them on the RAG path, with a
guardrails-off baseline that separates what the structure adds from what alignment
supplies, and a utility cost.



\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Agenda}
\label{sec:discussion}

The structural controls are the load-bearing ones, holding against the stated
threat model regardless of the model, and the factorial shows that the two attacks
they target close only when they are enabled, whatever the prompt; the remaining
attacks close through the heuristic controls, quarantine, and the secret-free prompt.
Several limitations bound the claims; we separate mechanism from empirical coverage.

The design carries three trade-offs. The retriever is a lexical (TF-IDF) baseline,
but retrieval authorisation acts on the candidate set independently of ranking, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:p2} is unaffected. The tool protocol is
textual, so an aligned model that declines the literal call syntax lowers
guardrails-off tool-abuse success, an alignment effect on the baseline that gateway-on
validation overrides. Output redaction strips the secret but the model may still echo
its secret-free hardened prompt, the residual noted in the scoring-validity check.

The empirical coverage is deliberately narrow: open-source models from 7B to 70B, no
commercial models, one synthetic corpus in one domain; because P2 and P5 act by
position, the caveat concerns how widely we measured, not the mechanism.
The attack battery is also narrow: one hand-crafted payload per OWASP class scored by
literal markers, so the cross-model ``zero'' is a class-representative demonstration,
not a per-class success rate; the payload-independent structural propositions carry
the class-level claim. The utility study is one author-written corpus and one
entitled-user probe, so it bounds over-blocking on realistic traffic rather than
estimating a precision; the redaction set is the five planted secret shapes, which
a deployment carrying legitimate account numbers would have to tune. Gateway-on cells are a single greedy run,
sampling-invariant for the structural closures (the offending content is removed
before generation) while the temperature-0.7 sweep covers the guardrails-off baseline
only. The platform integration is a feasibility result, not an operational study.

What remains open is the adaptive attacker; the agenda below is one the artifact
supports, since the policy object and pluggable corpus make each item a
configuration, not a rewrite. (1) \emph{Breadth of payloads}: loading the
BIPIA~\cite{yi2025bipia} indirect-injection corpus and the
AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} tasks through the corpus interface turns
the class-representative zero into per-class rates, and tests that the structural
closures stay at zero (they must, by Propositions~\ref{prop:p2} and~\ref{prop:p5})
while the heuristic ones degrade; visible-body payloads belong in the same battery.
(2) \emph{Adaptive red-teaming of the heuristics}: paraphrase, encoding, and
instruction-splitting variants against the released pattern sets, reporting not the
evasion rate, which Section~\ref{ssec:nonguar} predicts is high, but the impact
evasion buys once the structural controls cap it. (3) \emph{A3 as a testable
property}: Definition~\ref{def:fence} is a metamorphic test (vary only the fenced
region, check that tool calls are invariant), and running it per model yields a
fence-respect score for choosing models for P3, the first alignment property in
this setting that can be measured rather than trusted.
(4) \emph{Semantic retrieval and clearance-bound tools}: an embedding retriever
changes what enters the candidate set but not P2's filter on it, so the open
questions are embedding-space evasion of ranking and label population on a real
corpus, and, for P5, binding the recipient set to the clearances of the documents in
context so that the cross-principal deputy of Section~\ref{ssec:nonguar} is closed
structurally too. (5) \emph{Operational measurement}: the two over-blocking rates
and the entitled-access check of Section~\ref{ssec:utility} are the metrics a
deployment should log continuously; a controlled pilot on the integrated platform
would give them a base rate, a latency profile, and a head-to-head with an integrated
baseline such as SafeRAG~\cite{liang2025saferag} and a like-for-like injection
detector.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented an AI-aware security gateway that defends a RAG chatbot through five
controls applied around the model, its structural controls proved model-independent
and confirmed across open-source models, integrated with ROOTS as a feasibility
exercise, and shown to cost little utility. For an operator the message is to put
the security-relevant decisions in an auditable layer around the model: containment
by position is the guarantee, and the model's alignment is a bonus one can measure
but should not depend on.
