\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets chatbots answer from an organisation's
own documents, but it widens the attack surface: untrusted retrieved content and
user input reach the model and its tools directly, opening the door to prompt
injection, retrieval-time data leakage, and tool abuse. The usual defence trusts
the underlying model's safety alignment, which varies from model to model and is
hard to audit. We instead place the security-relevant decisions in a gateway
around the model, an AI-aware Policy Enforcement Point on the RAG pipeline that
applies five controls: three structural (retrieval authorisation and scoped tools,
which we prove sound for every model by their position in the pipeline, together
with context quarantine) and two heuristic (input filtering and output redaction, as
defence in depth). We
map the controls onto the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications and integrate the
gateway with a chatbot wired to a live tourism platform's document store and identity
provider, an integration we report as a feasibility result with no requests served
against live user data, and evaluate it on a controlled synthetic corpus across a
deterministic worst case and six open-source models (7B--70B). With the gateway on,
attack success is zero by our rubric for every model on all six evaluated attack
classes, while the guardrails-off baseline varies from model to model. A factorial
sweep shows the structural controls, not a hardened prompt, close the attacks
alignment misses (data leakage and tool abuse), and the gateway neutralises attacks
that two single-component guardrails, Llama Guard and NeMo Guardrails, miss (a
coverage-gap comparison, not a like-for-like benchmark). The safety these controls give is
model-independent by construction; we claim it empirically for open-source models
from 7B to 70B with lexical retrieval.

\keywords{RAG security \and prompt injection \and LLM guardrails \and OWASP LLM Top-10 \and AI gateway \and open-source LLMs.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become the default way to put a large
language model (LLM) to work over an organisation's own documents
\cite{lewis2020rag}: a user question is answered not from the model's weights
alone but from passages retrieved at query time from a private corpus. That design
is also where the security model breaks down, because two untrusted inputs now
reach the model and any tools it can call, namely the user's message and the
content of the documents the retriever returns. An attacker who controls either
one can try to override the system prompt, read documents they are not entitled
to, or drive a connected tool to act on their behalf. Direct and indirect prompt
injection, sensitive-information disclosure, and excessive agency sit among the
most prominent entries in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications
\cite{owasp2025llmtop10,greshake2023indirect,perez2022ignore}.

The common response is to rely on the model's own safety alignment to refuse
malicious instructions, but that is the wrong layer to depend on. Alignment is a
property of a specific model, it varies between models and versions, and an
operator can neither inspect nor guarantee it; a control that holds for one
open-source model may fail on the next, with nothing in the deployment making the
difference visible. We take the opposite position and place the security-relevant
decisions in a gateway around the model rather than in the model itself. The
gateway sits on the RAG pipeline as a Policy Enforcement Point and applies five
controls regardless of which model produces the answer. Two of them, retrieval
authorisation and scoped tools, are least-privilege guarantees that hold by their
position in the pipeline; a third, context quarantine, is partially structural;
the remaining two, input filtering and output redaction, are best-effort heuristic
checks that add defence in depth. Retrieval authorisation and scoped tools are
standard access-control practices, least privilege applied to retrieval and tool use
\cite{saltzer1975protection}: our contribution is not to invent controls but
to compose them into a model-independent gateway and to measure, across models,
how much safety comes from the gateway versus from a given model's alignment.

We evaluate six attack scenarios, one per addressed OWASP class: S1 direct prompt
injection, S2 indirect injection through a retrieved document, S3 RAG data leakage,
S4 tool abuse, S5 system-prompt leakage, and S6 improper output handling. The
paper makes three contributions: a model-independent gateway of five controls with a
formal threat model and security properties, two structural controls proved sound for
every model and context quarantine sound under one stated assumption, with the
heuristic controls' non-guarantees stated precisely
(Sections~\ref{sec:threat}--\ref{sec:owasp}); a feasibility integration of the gateway
with a chatbot wired to a live tourism platform's document store and identity provider,
not run on live user data, with the controlled evaluation on a synthetic corpus for
reproducibility (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}); and a cross-model evaluation
attributing the secure-mode safety to the structural controls rather than a hardened
prompt (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). That evaluation pairs the model-dependent
guardrails-off baseline against the uniform gateway-on behaviour and adds a full $2^5$
factorial sweep, external guardrail baselines, a second-scorer validity check, and a
negligible-overhead and false-positive analysis.

\section{Background and Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

A RAG chatbot answers a user message $m$ from a corpus $D$ of documents. A
retriever selects a small set $R \subseteq D$ relevant to $m$, the model generates
a response from the system prompt, $m$, and the text of $R$, and the response may
request calls to external tools. Both $m$ and the content of $R$ are
attacker-influenced, so every stage downstream of retrieval is an opportunity for
the attacker.

We model the deployment with users $U$ and documents $D$. Each user $u$ holds
clearances $\mathit{cl}(u)\subseteq C$ and each document $d$ a label
$\mathit{acl}(d)\in C$; $u$ may read $d$ only if $\mathit{acl}(d)\in\mathit{cl}(u)$,
and we write $A(u)=\{d\in D:\mathit{acl}(d)\in\mathit{cl}(u)\}$ for the documents $u$
is entitled to. The system prompt $\mathit{sys}$ and the gateway configuration are
trusted developer artefacts. The user message $m$ and the body of every retrieved
document are untrusted data: they may inform an answer but must never be promoted to
instructions.

The attacker aims to disclose a restricted document, execute an out-of-scope action,
exfiltrate the system prompt, or emit active content, and has two capabilities:
(C1) it submits arbitrary user messages, including ones that try to override the
system prompt or drive the tools (direct injection); and (C2) it plants arbitrary
content in documents the retriever may later surface, so that retrieved text carries
instructions (indirect injection and corpus
poisoning)~\cite{greshake2023indirect,zou2024poisonedrag}. It controls neither the
gateway configuration nor the access labels.

This is the confused-deputy problem~\cite{hardy1988confused}. Without the gateway the model is a deputy that
acts on whatever text reaches it, exercising the union of the developer's authority
(its tools) and any authority implied by untrusted content; injected text borrows
that authority. The gateway re-imposes the principal's authority at the pipeline
boundary, binding retrieval to $\mathit{cl}(u)$ before the deputy acts and tool
execution to a fixed scope after it, so that a misled deputy still cannot exceed the
authority enforced around it.

The guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:properties} rest on three assumptions, stated so
their scope is explicit. (A1) Access labels are tamper-proof and enforced by the
document store; if labels can be forged, retrieval authorisation no longer holds.
(A2) For the heuristic controls only, the attacker is non-adaptive: one who knows
the filters' patterns can craft obfuscated bypasses, and such evasion is out of
scope for P1 and P4, though not for the structural controls, which never inspect
content. (A3) For the quarantine guarantee, the model honours the untrusted-data
fence; we make this precise in Section~\ref{sec:properties} and show the reference
gateway satisfies it by construction.

The gateway enforces the five controls of Table~\ref{tab:props}, three structural
(P2, P3, P5) and two heuristic (P1, P4). The next two sections present the design
and then prove which guarantees hold for every model.

\section{The AI-Aware Security Gateway}
\label{sec:gateway}

The gateway is a single pipeline parameterised by a security policy
(Figure~\ref{fig:gateway}). In the vulnerable configuration every control is off
and a weak, secret-bearing system prompt is used, so the user message and
retrieved content flow straight to the model and its tools; in the secure
configuration all five controls are on and a hardened prompt is used. Expressing
the difference as one policy object rather than as branches in the code keeps the
before/after comparison exact and makes the gateway a drop-in component for any RAG
application. The controls act in pipeline order: the input filter rejects an
injecting message before retrieval; retrieval authorisation restricts the
candidate set to documents the user may read, so a restricted document is never
placed in the context; context quarantine fences what is retrieved; the model then
generates; output redaction strips secret and active content on the way out; and
scoped tools validate every requested call before executing it.

The design claim is that P2 and P5 do not depend on the model. Retrieval
authorisation filters candidates before the model runs, so even a model that fully
obeys an injected instruction cannot disclose a document it never received, and a
scoped tool refuses an out-of-policy call even if the model emits it verbatim.
Whether a model refuses a naive ``ignore your instructions'' prompt is, by
contrast, an alignment property that varies across models. The evaluation
quantifies both halves of this picture and, through the factorial sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, attributes the secure-mode result to the controls
rather than to the hardened prompt and isolates the marginal effect of each
individual control.

The gateway ships as a library whose only configuration surface is the policy
object, and four interfaces are pluggable so the same controls apply to different
deployments without touching the control logic: a corpus supplies documents with
access labels, a principals source maps an identity to its clearances, a model
provider abstracts the backend (the deterministic stub, a local Ollama model, or
any OpenAI-compatible endpoint), and an optional judge re-scores stored transcripts
offline. Section~\ref{sec:deployment} binds the corpus and principals interfaces to
a production platform, while the evaluation swaps only the model provider across
seven backends; the control implementations are byte-for-byte identical in every
case, which is what model-independence means in practice as well as in the proofs.

\section{Security Properties}
\label{sec:properties}

We give the gateway a semantics that makes precise which guarantees hold for every
model and which are conditional. A model is any function $M$ mapping an input triple
$(\mathit{sys},m,X)$ to a response $r$ and a set of tool calls $\mathcal{C}$. We
quantify over $M$, so a statement proved for all $M$ depends on no model's
alignment. On a request $(u,m)$ the secure pipeline retrieves
$R=\mathit{topk}(m,A(u))\subseteq A(u)$, fences it as $X=\mathit{quarantine}(R)$,
computes $(r,\mathcal{C})=M(\mathit{sys},m,X)$, releases $r'=\mathit{redact}(r)$, and
executes $\mathit{Exec}=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:\mathit{name}(c)\in\mathit{allow}\wedge
\mathit{args}(c)\in\mathit{scope}\}$. The quarantine transform wraps each chunk in an
untrusted-data fence, strips HTML comments, and neutralises embedded control
markers.

\subsection{Model-independent guarantees}

\begin{proposition}[P2, retrieval-authorisation soundness]
\label{prop:p2}
For every model $M$, user $u$, and message $m$, the model input contains content
only from documents in $A(u)$; consequently no document $d$ with
$\mathit{acl}(d)\notin\mathit{cl}(u)$ influences the response.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By A1 the access labels are authentic, so $A(u)$ is the true authorised set.
Retrieval restricts candidates to $A(u)$ before $M$ is invoked, so $R\subseteq A(u)$
and $M$'s input is a function only of $(\mathit{sys},m,\mathit{quarantine}(R))$. No
$d\notin A(u)$ appears in that input, for any $M$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[P5, tool-scope soundness]
\label{prop:p5}
For every model $M$ and output $(r,\mathcal{C})$, a call $c$ is executed only if
$\mathit{name}(c)\in\mathit{allow}$ and $\mathit{args}(c)\in\mathit{scope}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\mathit{Exec}$ filters $\mathcal{C}$ by the scope predicate before execution,
independent of how $\mathcal{C}$ was produced. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[P3, quarantine completeness]
\label{prop:p3c}
For every model $M$, user $u$, and message $m$, every retrieved chunk reaching the
model is enclosed in the untrusted-data fence and carries no marker from the set
that $\mathit{quarantine}$ neutralises.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The pipeline applies $\mathit{quarantine}$ to every element of $R$ before forming
$X$; by construction $\mathit{quarantine}$ wraps its argument in the fence and
removes the neutralised markers, independent of $M$. \qed
\end{proof}

Propositions~\ref{prop:p2}--\ref{prop:p3c} hold by the position of the control in the
pipeline, before $M$ (P2, P3) or after it (P5), which is the precise sense in which
they are model-independent. P2 and P5 are sound regardless of the model: a document
the user may not read never enters the model input, and a call outside scope is never
executed, whatever $M$ emits. P5 bounds the \emph{shape} of an executed call, its name
and arguments lie in scope; it does not bound the information a within-scope call may
carry, a limit we make explicit in Section~\ref{ssec:nonguar}. The confused deputy can
be misled, but it cannot exceed the authority the gateway enforces at its boundaries.

\subsection{A conditional guarantee for quarantine}

Quarantine completeness is structural, but whether fencing actually stops an embedded
instruction depends on the model honouring the fence. We isolate that dependence in a
single assumption. The fenced region may legitimately inform the answer; what must not
happen is for it to change the controlled actions the model takes.

\begin{definition}[Fence-respecting model]
\label{def:fence}
Partition the model's output into a \emph{controlled channel}, the tool calls
$\mathcal{C}$ together with a typed action label (for example, reveal the system
prompt or comply with an embedded directive), and a free-text answer $r$. $M$ is
\emph{fence-respecting} if its controlled channel is invariant under changes confined
to the fenced region: for any $X,X'$ that agree outside the fence,
$M(\mathit{sys},m,X)$ and $M(\mathit{sys},m,X')$ emit the same tool calls and the same
controlled actions. The free-text answer $r$ may differ, as it may legitimately use
the fenced content.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[P3, quarantine soundness under A3]
\label{prop:p3s}
If $M$ is fence-respecting, then no content in a retrieved document changes $M$'s tool
calls or controlled actions: a controlled action the request would not otherwise take
cannot be induced through retrieved content.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:p3c} every retrieved chunk reaching $M$ lies inside the
fence. Replacing the embedded content with inert text is a change confined to the
fenced region, so by Definition~\ref{def:fence} the controlled channel is unchanged. \qed
\end{proof}

The reference gateway realises a fence-respecting model by construction, in the sense
of tool-call and controlled-action invariance: its deterministic backend applies a
fence-stripping step that removes the fenced region before scanning for commands, so
Assumption A3 holds exactly and the worst-case
results of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} are a sound upper bound on compromise. Whether
a given production model satisfies A3 is an empirical, alignment-dependent question;
this is why we class P3 as partially structural and pair it with output redaction, and
why P5 still caps a non-fence-respecting model that does emit an injected call.

\subsection{Non-guarantees: the heuristic controls}
\label{ssec:nonguar}

The input filter P1 rejects a message matching a finite injection-pattern set $\Pi$,
and output redaction P4 substitutes a finite set $\Sigma$ of secret and
active-content shapes in the response. Neither is sound. For any finite $\Pi$ there
are semantically equivalent messages outside it (paraphrase, homoglyph substitution,
instruction splitting), and for any finite $\Sigma$ there are secret or
active-content encodings it does not match; an attacker who knows the patterns can
evade them, which is Assumption A2 made explicit. P1 and P4 are therefore defence in
depth, not guarantees. The structural controls bound the damage regardless: by
Propositions~\ref{prop:p2} and~\ref{prop:p5} an attacker who evades P1 and P4 still
cannot cause the model to receive an unauthorised document or to execute an
out-of-scope tool call. Scoped tools share this character at the margin: P5 refuses any
out-of-scope call, but a within-scope call (an internal recipient, a body within the
size cap) can still carry bounded data, so P5 limits rather than eliminates
tool-mediated disclosure; the S4 result of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures the
out-of-scope (external-recipient) attack that P5 provably refuses. This division of
labour is the design principle of the gateway: the heuristics reduce incidence, the
structural controls cap impact.

\section{Deployment and Evaluation Corpus}
\label{sec:deployment}

We integrate the gateway in front of a chatbot on a tourism-services platform, a
microservice system whose knowledge-base service exposes documents over a REST API
and whose identity is managed by a Keycloak realm (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). A new
chatbot microservice imports the gateway as a library and sits behind the platform's
nginx reverse proxy on a dedicated route. It binds the gateway's two deployment
interfaces to the platform: the corpus interface loads documents from the
knowledge-base REST API, and the principals interface validates the caller's
Keycloak bearer token against the realm's JWKS and maps the realm roles it carries
to gateway clearances, so retrieval authorisation (P2) runs on real identities. A
document's access label is carried by an \texttt{acl} field added to the
knowledge-base record schema. Only these two adapters are platform-specific; the
five control implementations are the same code exercised in the evaluation. This
demonstrates that the gateway slots into a real document store and identity provider
without changes to the control logic. It is an integration and feasibility result:
no integrated request was served against live user data, and we report no
operational metrics, latency, or real-user study from the deployment.

The controlled evaluation that follows instead uses a synthetic corpus, so that
the comparison is identical and reproducible across models and free of live-data

injection payloads for S2, S4, and S6. The structural guarantees (P2, P5) hold

on document content; what the synthetic setting does not exercise is operational
correctness of the live platform's label population, which we leave to future work.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}
The question is whether composing the controls yields model-independent safety,
and how the guardrails-off baseline varies across models. We run the six scenarios
in two modes, vulnerable (every control off, the baseline) and secure (the gateway
on), over a deterministic stub model and six open-source instruct models:
Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen2.5~7B, Mistral~7B, Gemma~3~12B, and Phi-4~14B served locally
through Ollama, and Llama~3.1~70B on a rented A40 GPU. The main grid covers all six
models in both modes; the factorial sweep, dose-response, and temperature-0.7
analyses that follow cover the five locally-served models (7B--14B) and the stub,
with the remotely-served 70B entering only the main single-run grid as a scale
spot-check. Decoding is greedy (temperature~0, fixed seed) so each cell is
deterministic and reported directly; a temperature-0.7 run later checks that the
findings survive sampling. The stub is a naive instruction-follower with no
refusal mechanism that echoes whatever an injection asks for, fixing the worst
case and making the controls' effect reproducible. We report, per (model,
scenario, mode), the attack success rate (ASR) and the refusal rate (the model
declined, an alignment signal). Structural controls do not all register as an
explicit block: P1 and P5 hard-reject observably, whereas P2 and P3 prevent the
outcome by suppression (the unauthorised document or instruction never reaches the
model), which shows up as reduced ASR rather than a separate signal. Outcomes are
scored by a transparent deterministic rubric keyed to each scenario's markers and
specified in the released code.\footnote{Code, rubric, and data:
\url{https://github.com/dferrero17/rag-sec-gateway}.}

\subsection{Model-independent safety and the alignment baseline}
With the gateway on, ASR is zero by the rubric for every model on every scenario.
This uniform closure is an empirical observation about the full gateway, not a single
guarantee: only data leakage (S3) and tool abuse (S4) follow from the
model-independent structural controls (P2, P5); direct injection (S1) is caught by the
input filter or redaction, system-prompt leakage (S5) by the hardened prompt and
redaction, and the indirect attacks (S2, S6) by quarantine, whose soundness is
conditional on the fence-respecting assumption (Section~\ref{sec:properties}). The
structural part holds for every model by construction; the cross-model data is what
makes the rest credible. With guardrails off the picture inverts
(Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}): the unaligned stub is compromised on all six
scenarios, while the six aligned models are compromised inconsistently, with mean
vulnerable ASR ranging from $0.17$ (Llama~3.1~8B, Phi-4~14B) to $0.50$
(Mistral~7B). The variation is attack-specific rather than a clean model ranking,
since only data leakage (S3) and tool abuse (S4) differ across models while S1,
S2, and S6 are handled by every aligned model. Scale does not buy safety:
Llama~3.1~70B (mean $0.33$) leaks the restricted document on S3 where its 8B
sibling refuses. System-prompt leakage (S5) is the exception that proves the rule:
it succeeds in vulnerable mode on all seven models, including the largest and best
aligned, because the oblique request slips past the alignment that refuses the
blatant variant (S1). The gap between the two modes is the safety
the gateway adds on top of whatever alignment a model happens to provide, and it
is the same gap for every model.

\subsection{Scoring validity}
Three checks confirm the secure result is not an artefact of the scorer, the
prompt, or the decoder. Two of the authors independently annotated all 72 cells
from the stored outputs, blind to the rubric's label; they agree on 67 (93\%;
Cohen's $\kappa$ = \humankappa, almost perfect), and the per-cell two-annotator labels
are in the released artifact. The five disagreements are all in
secure mode (four on S5, one on S3), where one annotator counted the model echoing
its secret-free hardened prompt, after the override code was redacted, as a partial
disclosure while the other and the rubric did not. This is a definitional choice
about residual disclosure rather than an attack the gateway let through; a stricter
no-disclosure rubric would also redact the prompt scaffold. As a second,
independent scorer we re-scored every stored output with an LLM judge
(Llama~3.1~8B reading each transcript and the scenario goal); judge and rubric agree
on 63 of 72 cells (88\%, Table~\ref{tab:scorer}), and the judge confirms the zero
attack successes the rubric assigns in secure mode, so neither scorer is the source
of the result.

\subsection{Attribution: which lever closes which attack}
The factorial sweep separates the prompt from the controls
(Table~\ref{tab:factorial}). Hardening the prompt alone leaves the structural
attacks untouched: data leakage and tool abuse stay open until retrieval
authorisation and scoped tools are added, which close them regardless of the
prompt, whereas system-prompt leakage is closed by the hardened prompt and not by
P2 or P5. The hardened prompt does not merely fail on data leakage but slightly
worsens it (S3 rises from $0.67$ to $0.83$), which is exactly why data leakage needs a
structural control rather than prompt engineering. The two levers are therefore
orthogonal, and the structural controls are the ones that act where alignment fails. A full $2^5$ sweep over every control
combination isolates each control (Appendix~\ref{app:isolation}): on the worst-case
stub, enabling one control at a time closes each attack with the control mapped to
it, and shows that system-prompt leakage is closed only by output redaction, not by
the input filter, since the oblique request bypasses it. A pre-redaction re-scoring supports the
same reading: in secure mode the model's output before the filter is already clean
for every real model on S5 and S6, so quarantine and the hardened prompt prevent
the leak upstream and the output filter is only a backstop, relied on solely by the
naive stub.

quarantine least, so the per-control ranking is corpus- and model-dependent. Either
way, each control enabled alone still leaves attacks open and only the full set
reaches zero: the controls compose rather than substitute, the layered design the

\subsection{Comparison with off-the-shelf guardrails}
Two off-the-shelf guardrail systems cover far less of this surface. Llama Guard~3
\cite{inan2023llamaguard}, a content-safety classifier, flags none of the six
attack inputs, which read as benign, and only three of the six worst-case outputs,
missing data leakage, tool abuse, and prompt leakage; NeMo Guardrails'
\cite{rebedea2023nemo} self-check input rail, backed by Llama~3.1~8B, blocks only
the blatant direct injection, one of six. Both are single-component classifiers
rather than full pipelines, so the comparison shows that isolated classifiers miss
structural attacks, not a like-for-like ranking. Re-running the guardrails-off
baseline at temperature~0.7 with ten seeded samples per cell leaves the picture
unchanged: the cross-model variation persists (mean vulnerable ASR $0.07$--$0.60$)
and sampling surfaces, if anything, more successes. The sampled sweep covers the
vulnerable baseline only; secure-mode cleanliness rests on the single greedy
generation plus the deterministic redaction backstop, which sampling cannot perturb.

\subsection{Operational cost and false positives}
The structural controls are pure functions, so the gateway adds negligible latency.
Timing each control in isolation over $2\times10^{5}$ calls gives medians of
$0.3$--$0.9\,\mu$s for the input filter, retrieval authorisation, quarantine, and
tool validation, and $5.3\,\mu$s for output redaction (the most regex-heavy),
Figure~\ref{fig:overhead}; the five together cost about $7.5\,\mu$s per query, four
to six orders of magnitude below the per-model median inference time of
$1.5$--$7.6$\,s we measured (Llama~3.1~8B to Phi-4~14B).
The two content-inspection controls can in principle over-block, so we ran a corpus
of 45 benign tourism questions (including near-misses such as ``booking system'' and
``follow the instructions on the sign'') through the secure pipeline: the input
filter rejected none, and output redaction altered none of the answers
(0/45 false positives on both, the input filter exactly so as it is deterministic
and model-independent). The corpus is small and single-domain, so we read this as
evidence the patterns are not trigger-happy rather than a precision guarantee.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Prompt injection was characterised by Perez and Ribeiro \cite{perez2022ignore} for
the direct case and by Greshake et al.\ \cite{greshake2023indirect} for the indirect
case, where instructions hidden in retrieved content hijack an LLM-integrated
application; HouYi \cite{liu2024houyi} turned this into a black-box attack framework
against deployed applications, and Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2024promptinjection} give the
first systematic cross-model benchmark. The surface has since been charted by
crowd-sourced attack corpora \cite{toyer2024tensortrust}, indirect-injection
benchmarks \cite{yi2025bipia}, and automatic, transferable payloads produced by
optimisation \cite{liu2024universalinjection}. These works define the classes the
gateway must intercept; we contribute not a new attack but a structural layer
measured against them with a guardrails-off baseline.

One line of defence hardens the model itself. StruQ \cite{chen2024struq} and SecAlign
\cite{chen2024secalign} separate instructions from data through structured queries
and preference optimisation, spotlighting marks provenance \cite{hines2024spotlighting},
Jatmo \cite{piet2024jatmo} fine-tunes a model to its task so injected instructions are
not followed, and the instruction hierarchy \cite{wallace2024instructionhierarchy}
trains a model to prioritise privileged messages. These defences are per-model and
re-trainable, and recent work shows alignment can be shallow \cite{qi2024shallowsafety}
or countered only at the representation level \cite{zou2024circuitbreakers}. The
gateway's controls sit outside the model, so the same code applies unchanged across
backends, which we verify cross-model.

A second line constrains the system around the model. Design-pattern catalogues
\cite{beurerkellner2025designpatterns} and the dual-LLM pattern
\cite{willison2023dualllm} isolate untrusted content from privileged action, while
CaMeL \cite{debenedetti2025camel}, information-flow control \cite{wu2024fsecure},
type-directed privilege separation \cite{jacob2025typedirected}, and execution
isolation \cite{wu2025isolategpt} enforce least privilege by construction. The gateway
shares this secure-by-design stance but targets the RAG retrieval-and-generation path
with lightweight controls and reports a guardrails-off baseline that quantifies what
the structural layer adds.

RAG makes the corpus a first-class attack surface. The datastore can be extracted
verbatim through injected instructions \cite{qi2025spillbeans} or probed for
membership \cite{anderson2025ismydata}, its privacy tension has been characterised
\cite{zeng2024goodbad}, and it can be poisoned \cite{zou2024poisonedrag} or backdoored
\cite{chaudhari2024phantom}; SafeRAG \cite{liang2025saferag} benchmarks pipelines
against several such attacks and is the natural integrated comparison target. Model
self-critique such as Self-RAG \cite{asai2024selfrag} complements, rather than
replaces, the structural mediation we add at the retrieval boundary.

Tool-using agents widen the surface again. InjecAgent \cite{zhan2024injecagent} and
AgentDojo \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} benchmark indirect injection and defences
for agents, AgentHarm \cite{andriushchenko2025agentharm} measures harmful tool use,
and adaptive attacks break single-layer agent defences \cite{zhan2025adaptiveipi},
which is the empirical case for defence in depth and for structural controls that do
not inspect content. Guardrail classifiers such as NeMo Guardrails
\cite{rebedea2023nemo}, Llama Guard \cite{inan2023llamaguard}, and Granite Guardian
\cite{padhi2024graniteguardian}, and adaptive security services \cite{pfister2025gandalf},
operate alongside such a layer rather than replacing it, as our baseline comparison
shows. We align the threat model with the OWASP LLM Top-10 \cite{owasp2025llmtop10},
the NIST AI RMF \cite{nist2023airmf}, and MITRE ATLAS \cite{mitre2024atlas}.
Table~\ref{tab:positioning} positions representative approaches: ours is the only row
that is model-independent, evaluated cross-model against a guardrails-off baseline,
and RAG-aware at once.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The structural controls are the load-bearing ones, holding against the stated
threat model regardless of the model, and the factorial confirms the secure result
comes from them rather than from the hardened prompt. Several limitations bound the
claims, and we separate those that concern the mechanism from those that concern
the empirical coverage.

The design carries three trade-offs. The retriever is a lexical (TF-IDF) baseline,
though retrieval authorisation acts on the candidate set independently of how it is
ranked, so the guarantee of Proposition~\ref{prop:p2} is unaffected by the choice of
retriever. The tool protocol is textual, so an aligned model that declines the
literal call syntax lowers vulnerable-mode tool-abuse success; this is an alignment
effect on the baseline, and secure-mode validation overrides it regardless. Output
redaction strips the secret but the model may still echo its secret-free hardened
prompt, which one annotator counted as a partial leak, a definitional choice about
residual disclosure rather than an attack the gateway let through.

The empirical coverage is deliberately narrow. All models are open-source, from 7B
to 70B, with no commercial models, and the evaluation runs on a controlled synthetic
corpus in a single domain. Because P2 and P5 act by position rather than by
inspecting content, the structural argument does not depend on model scale or domain,
so this caveat concerns how widely we have measured rather than the mechanism itself;
the false-positive probe of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} is one small corpus and a
larger multi-domain study would sharpen the precision claim.

What remains genuinely open is the adaptive attacker. Assumption A2 excludes an
adversary who knows the input-filter and redaction patterns and crafts obfuscated
bypasses; such an attacker can defeat P1 and P4, and the honest consequence is that
the heuristic layer reduces incidence while the structural controls, which never
inspect content, still cap the impact (Section~\ref{sec:properties}). A standing
adaptive red-team against the heuristics, a semantic retriever with its own
embedding-space attack surface, an integrated RAG-security baseline such as
SafeRAG~\cite{liang2025saferag}, and live operational metrics on a production
deployment are the natural next steps.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented an AI-aware security gateway that defends a RAG chatbot through five
controls applied around the model, integrated it as a feasibility deployment with a
real platform's document store and identity provider (without serving live user data),
and evaluated it on a controlled synthetic corpus across open-source models against six
OWASP LLM Top-10 attack classes. With the
gateway on, attack success was zero by our rubric for every model on every
scenario, while the guardrails-off baseline varied from model to model, and a
factorial sweep attributed the result to the structural controls rather than to a
hardened prompt. For an operator the message is to put the security-relevant
decisions in an auditable layer around the model rather than in the model itself.
